India got its greenium. It just had to stop selling bonds to keep it.

Neil Sangwaiya  ·  8 August 2026  ·  Independent research note, v1.0
Data verified to 8 August 2026  ·  SSRN abstract 7251660

Across seven auctions where a conventional Government of India comparator could be matched, India's sovereign green bonds priced an average of 2.0 basis points below the conventional curve. The programme has lowered the government's borrowing cost, but by an amount worth roughly ₹29 crore a year against an interest bill of ₹9.28 lakh crore. The more consequential finding is how that pricing was held.

On 31 May 2024 the Reserve Bank of India offered ₹6,000 crore of 10-year sovereign green bonds. Investors bid about twice that. The RBI sold nothing. Two months later it tried again with the same notified amount and accepted ₹1,697 crore, roughly 28 per cent. By the end of that financial year the government's dated securities programme was short by ₹10,302.602 crore, and both the Clearing Corporation of India and the Finance Ministry's own debt reports pin the shortfall on green bond auctions where the greenium on offer was too low.

I went into this expecting to find out whether India's green bonds priced below the conventional curve. They did, a bit. What I did not expect was that the number would matter so much less than how it was obtained.

I started from the Department of Economic Affairs' Public Debt Management quarterly reports, which publish every dated security auction with its date, notified amount, amount raised, devolvement on primary dealers, cut-off price and cut-off yield. That gives me the green bond side and the conventional side from one primary source, on identical terms. For each green bond auction I picked the nearest-maturity conventional Government of India security, took its cut-off yield at the auction immediately before and immediately after, interpolated linearly to the green bond's auction date, and subtracted.

Seven auctions produced a usable comparison. Mean greenium: +2.0 basis points. Median: also +2.0. Weighted by the amount actually placed with investors rather than pushed onto primary dealers, it drops to about 1.8 bps.

That is not nothing, and it lines up with two independent sources. The Finance Ministry says the January and February 2023 debut issues went at a greenium of 2 to 6 bps. IEEFA puts India's historical figure at 2 to 3 bps. My calculation lands inside both. When three different methods agree, I am reasonably confident the real number is small, positive, and in the low single digits.

Now the interesting part.

Where the greenium went

In November and December 2023, in back-to-back auctions, the 5-year and 10-year green bonds both priced at exactly the interpolated conventional yield. Zero bps. Twice. The 5-year SGrB 2028 cleared at 7.25 per cent when the 7.37% GS 2028 was auctioned at 7.29 per cent a week earlier and 7.21 per cent a week later. The 10-year SGrB 2033 cleared at 7.24 per cent between conventional prints of 7.29 and 7.19. By the second year of the programme, the green label was worth zero on paper the market actually wanted to buy in size.

Then it got worse and better at the same time. The two largest 10-year greeniums I measured, 2.0 bps in August 2024 and 3.3 bps in November 2024, were bought at a price. In August the government sold 28 per cent of what it offered. In November it sold ₹5,000 crore on paper, but ₹3,498 crore of that devolved on primary dealers.

Devolvement is worth explaining properly, because it is easy to misread. Primary dealers underwrite Indian government auctions. If the RBI decides the yields investors are demanding are too high, it accepts less than the notified amount and the balance goes to the underwriters at the cut-off. So devolvement does not mean nobody wanted the bonds. It means nobody wanted them at the price the government was willing to accept. The demand curve existed. It just sat above the reservation yield.

A cut-off yield is normally the market-clearing price for the full notified amount. When the issuer truncates the quantity, it stops being a clearing price and becomes a reservation price.

That reframes the entire measurement. You are no longer measuring what investors would pay. You are measuring what the government would accept. Every basis point of greenium calculated on a devolved or partially rejected auction is, strictly speaking, an upper bound.

There is direct evidence for this. IEEFA reports that at the cancelled May 2024 auction, bids came in 1 to 7 bps above the 6.99 per cent benchmark. At full size, in other words, the green bond carried a negative greenium. Investors wanted paying extra.

Tenor is the variable that matters

The one clean observation is the 30-year. On 13 December 2024 the 6.98% SGrB 2054 sold its full ₹5,000 crore at 6.98 per cent against an interpolated 7.0267 per cent on the 7.09% GS 2054. That is +4.7 bps, fully placed, no devolvement, no rejection. Insurers and pension funds buying 30-year duration have a genuine scarcity problem, and green paper at that maturity gives them something conventional supply does not. The government appears to have drawn the same conclusion: its H1 FY2025-26 borrowing calendar put green issuance entirely in the 30-year bucket.

So what is it worth?

Take the top of my measured range, 5 bps, and apply it to the ₹57,697.4 crore raised across FY2022-23 to FY2024-25. That is about ₹29 crore a year of interest saved. Central government interest payments in FY2022-23 were ₹9.28 lakh crore. The green label is saving roughly three-thousandths of one per cent of the interest bill.

Where the money went

The FY2023-24 allocation report is unusually specific, which makes it useful. Of ₹20,785.60 crore transferred, ₹16,087.99 crore (77.4 per cent) went to clean transportation, ₹4,578.47 crore (22.0 per cent) to renewable energy, and ₹119.14 crore (0.6 per cent) to the National Mission for a Green India. The single largest line is ₹9,928.99 crore for production of energy-efficient three-phase electric locomotives, which is 48 per cent of the whole programme.

Two things stand out. First, the report's own summary chart shows renewable energy at ₹6,116.3 crore and 27 per cent, while its detailed table shows ₹4,578.47 crore and 22.0 per cent. A ₹1,537.83 crore gap between two figures in the same document is a reporting quality problem, not a scandal, but it is the kind of thing an investor doing diligence would flag.

Second, and more substantively: in both reported years the government spent more on eligible schemes than the bonds raised, and funded the excess from general revenue. ₹16,923 crore against ₹16,000 crore in FY2022-23. ₹20,785.60 crore against ₹20,000 crore in FY2023-24. Proceeds go into the Consolidated Fund of India and are allocated to metro equity injections, locomotive procurement, PM-KUSUM, rooftop solar and the Green India Mission, all of which were already budgeted.

That is not a breach of anything. India's framework explicitly permits financing and refinancing. But it does mean the honest description is that the green bond re-labelled expenditure rather than creating it. Eligibility is demonstrated. Allocation is demonstrated. Additionality is not, and was never promised.

What CICERO actually said

CICERO Shades of Green rated the framework "Medium Green" with a "Good" governance score. Both terms are narrower than they sound. Medium Green in CICERO's methodology means projects that represent significant steps towards the long-term vision but are not quite there yet. Good is the middle of three governance grades, below Excellent.

CICERO reviewed the framework document. Not the bonds, not the auctions, not the projects, not the outcomes. It graded renewable energy, energy efficiency, clean transportation and adaptation medium-to-dark green, and gave light or light-to-medium shading to sustainable water and waste management and to pollution prevention, because those categories were written broadly with no numerical thresholds. It also recommended an independent third-party audit of the controls over project selection, management of proceeds, and allocation and impact reporting.

A second party opinion tells an investor that the rulebook is credible. It says nothing about whether the rules were followed, and nothing about whether a tonne of carbon was avoided that would otherwise have been emitted. Treating it as proof of impact is a category error.

The judgement

India's sovereign green bond programme has demonstrated that a rupee-denominated sovereign green curve can be built, sold, listed and reported on. It has produced a measurable financing advantage of roughly 2 basis points on average, concentrated at the long end and worth a rounding error against the sovereign interest bill.

It has not demonstrated that investors will pay a premium at scale. Four failed or truncated auctions between May 2024 and June 2025 are the market's answer to that question, and the ₹10,302.602 crore borrowing shortfall is the price the Treasury paid for insisting. And it has not demonstrated additionality, because it was not designed to.

The case for continuing is real. It is just not the case that is usually made. The programme is worth running as market infrastructure: a benchmark curve, a domestic investor base, a reporting discipline that did not exist before. It is not worth running as a cheap funding strategy, because at 2 basis points it isn't one.
